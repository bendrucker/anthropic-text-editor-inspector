# Halvorsen Cannery Records

The collection documents a salmon cannery at Ketchum Point from its first pack in 1911 through the sale of the plant in 1979. It reached the archive in two accessions eleven years apart. This report treats them as one body of material, because the second accession turned out to contain the missing half of series that had already been arranged and described from the first. Processing finished in March 2011.

## Provenance

The cannery operated under three owners and two names. Halvorsen and Sons ran it until 1948, the Pacific Northern Packing Company through 1971, and a holding company with no local presence for the final eight years. Records from all three periods were kept in the same office, and the filing practices carried over largely unchanged across both sales, which is why the arrangement below follows function rather than ownership.

The first accession came from the family in 1994. A granddaughter of the founder had kept sixty boxes in a heated garage since the plant closed, and she transferred them along with a typed inventory her father had made. That inventory is now filed at the front of the collection. It is wrong about nearly every date it gives, and it is valuable anyway, because it records where each group of files sat in the office. Matching those positions against the boxes recovered the original order of the label files and the union files, both of which had been shelved out of sequence.

The second accession arrived in 2005 from the county historical society, which had taken the contents of the plant office when the building was condemned. Nobody there had processed it. The boxes arrived on three flatbed trucks, still in the steel shelving they had been pulled out on, and the shelving had to be cut apart in the loading bay before anything could be moved indoors.

## Condition on Arrival

The family material arrived dry and intact. The historical society material was neither.

Roughly a third of the second accession shows water damage from a roof failure that predates the condemnation. The damage sits in the lower two shelves throughout, which points to standing water rather than a leak from above, and it is worst in the payroll ledgers, whose bindings absorbed the moisture and held it. Several ledgers had blocked into solid masses that cannot be opened without splitting the text block.

Mold was active in eleven boxes. Those were isolated on arrival, frozen, and treated before anything else in the accession was touched. Six came out of treatment with the paper intact and the ink legible. The other five lost most of a season's shipment ledgers each. Those gaps are named under Ledgers and Payroll rather than passed over, since the annual pack totals do not reconcile across the years they cover.

## Appraisal

About four hundred cubic feet arrived. Two hundred and ten remain.

Most of what went was routine and duplicated. The cannery kept carbon copies of outgoing correspondence in two places, filed by date in the office and by correspondent in the manager's own cabinet, and the two sets overlap almost completely for the years both survive. The date-ordered set was kept because it is continuous. The manager's set was sampled at five-year intervals and the rest destroyed.

Bulk also came out of the fisheries reporting files, which hold state-mandated forms filed in triplicate for forty years. One copy of each was kept. The state archive holds complete runs of the forms themselves, so even the retained copies are here for their annotations rather than their content. Several carry marginal notes on gear and crew that appear nowhere else in the collection.

Damage alone was never grounds for a discard. Two boxes went out because nothing in them could be read by any means the archive has, and both were photographed before disposal.

## Arrangement

The collection is arranged in five series, all of them divisions the office itself used. Where the original order survived it was kept, including where it is inconvenient. The correspondence runs on a fiscal year that starts in April, and splitting a calendar year across two boxes was preferred to reordering files the cannery had kept one way for six decades.

### Correspondence

The largest series, and the one most likely to be used. It runs 1911 to 1974, with a gap from 1943 to 1946 that is a loss rather than a lapse in filing, since the letter books resume mid-thread.

The early letters are the most interesting and the hardest to use. Before 1930 the outgoing copies are letterpress tissues, made by pressing damp tissue against a freshly written original and binding the result. Many have faded past reading. The ones that survive are legible only when the tissue is backed with white card, and the explanation comes up in the reading room most weeks.

### Ledgers and Payroll

Twenty-eight bound volumes covering wages, advances against wages, the company store, and season shipment totals. The store accounts are why the series matters. Workers were paid partly in credit, and the ledgers track what individual families bought and owed across whole seasons, which makes them the closest thing here to a record of daily life at the plant.

Names in these volumes are recorded inconsistently. Chinese and Filipino crew are often entered by a contractor's number instead of a name, and the same worker can appear under three spellings in three years. Any sustained research use will need a reconciliation the finding aid cannot do in advance.

Five seasons are missing from the shipment ledgers, lost to the mold in the second accession. The summary volumes survive for those years, and their annual pack totals cannot be reconciled against the underlying records.

### Photographic Material

About twelve hundred images, of which roughly nine hundred are negatives. Two hundred and forty of the negatives are cellulose nitrate.

The nitrate was identified during processing and moved to cold storage the same week. It is not available for consultation. Duplication is underway at the rate the budget allows, which is slower than the deterioration in the worst of it. The negatives already in visible decay were copied first regardless of what they show.

The prints are largely unlabeled. Where a boat, a building, or a piece of equipment could be identified from the correspondence or the ledgers, the identification is recorded in the finding aid together with the evidence for it. A later researcher is free to disagree.

### Label and Package Design

A small series and a heavily requested one. It holds can labels from every brand the plant packed, including four packed under contract for buyers who appear nowhere else in the records. The artwork files include proofs carrying printer's corrections, the only place in the collection where an outside supplier's working papers survive.

### Union and Grievance Files

Records of the plant's dealings with two unions between 1937 and 1971. The grievance files name individual workers and describe conduct, injury, and discipline. Restrictions on this series are set out below.

The negotiation files are open and are the better source anyway for anyone working on labor rather than on a particular person. They include the company's own bargaining notes, which are candid in a way the signed agreements are not, and a run of internal memoranda from the 1952 strike that reads as a day-by-day account of a dispute the newspapers covered only at its start and its end.

## Preservation

Beyond the mold treatment, work was limited to rehousing, flattening, and pulling fasteners. Nothing was deacidified. The newsprint clippings interleaved through the correspondence were photocopied and the originals moved to a separate box rather than left to burn the letters they sat against.

Four ledgers need rebinding before they can be handled at all. They are described in the finding aid and marked unavailable, which is better than leaving a researcher to conclude they are gone.

## Restrictions

The grievance files are closed for seventy-five years from the date of each file, following the archive's standard for personnel records. The earliest opened in 2012 and the last closes in 2046. This was not negotiated with the donor, who had no authority over material the historical society transferred.

Everything else is open. The photographic material is open subject to the nitrate handling rule, which is a safety restriction rather than an access one, and researchers are given duplicates where duplicates exist.

## Related Collections

Three other holdings bear directly on this one. The state archive keeps the fisheries reporting series the cannery filed into, in complete runs and with better indexing than anything here. The maritime museum two counties north holds the papers of the tender fleet that ran fish to the plant, which covers the same seasons from the water rather than the shore. The historical society retained a set of oral histories recorded with former workers in the 1980s, and those interviews are the only place where several of the names in the payroll ledgers are attached to a person rather than a number.

A researcher working on the plant's last decade will find little here. The holding company kept its records elsewhere and disposed of them after the sale, and what survives from those years is operational paper the office generated for itself.

## Outstanding Work

Item-level description of the photographs is unfinished and will stay unfinished under current staffing. Finishing it would take about four months of one archivist's time, and the series is usable now at folder level.

The 1943 to 1946 correspondence gap deserves one more search. Three boxes from the second accession were never fully identified, and their format matches the missing years even though the labels on them say otherwise.
