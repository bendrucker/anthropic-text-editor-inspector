# The Monotype Composition Caster

A note on why the Monotype system survived in book work long after the Linotype had taken over newspapers, and why the two machines produced such different pages.

## Two Answers to the Same Problem

Both machines automated the setting of metal type, and both appeared within a few years of each other. They solved the problem in ways that were nearly opposite.

The Linotype cast a whole line as a single slug. An operator sat at a keyboard, assembled a line of brass matrices, and the machine cast that line complete. Correcting a single letter meant recasting the entire line, which for newspaper work was no burden at all. Speed mattered and the page was thrown away the same day.

The Monotype split the work in two. A keyboard operator produced a punched paper ribbon, and a separate caster read that ribbon and cast individual pieces of type, one letter at a time, into a galley. The output was ordinary loose type, indistinguishable in handling from type set by hand.

## Why Book Printers Chose Monotype

The division of labor is what made the difference. Because the Monotype produced individual sorts, a compositor could correct a single wrong letter with tweezers rather than recasting anything. In a book that would be proofread three times, that mattered enormously.

The Monotype also handled complex composition that defeated a line-casting machine. Mathematics, tabular matter, and mixed alphabets all require pieces of type that do not sit on a common line, and a machine that casts a whole line as one slug cannot easily produce them. Scholarly presses stayed with Monotype for exactly this reason well into the era when the technology was otherwise obsolete.

## The Ribbon

The punched ribbon is the part of the Monotype system that reads as most modern. The keyboard produced no type at all. It produced instructions, and those instructions could be stored, duplicated, or carried to a different caster in a different building.

A press could keyboard a book in one city and cast it in another. A standing correction could be punched once and run whenever a reprint was needed, without keeping tons of metal standing in a warehouse. The ribbon separated the intellectual work of composition from the physical work of casting, which is a separation the Linotype never made.

## The End of It

Photocomposition ended both machines within about a decade, and the Monotype went first in commercial work despite its advantages, because its two-stage process needed two operators and more floor space. What kept it alive longest was the work nothing else could do well: mathematics, critical editions, and any page where a single letter might need to change after the type was set.
